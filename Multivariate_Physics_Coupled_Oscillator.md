# 1. Introduction: Why do we need multivariable calculus?

In elementary mechanics, we often describe the motion of a particle using a single coordinate $x(t)$. Its equation of motion is

$$
\ddot{x}=f(x,\dot{x},t).
$$

However, many physical systems involve several degrees of freedom. For example:

- Two masses connected by springs.
- A double pendulum.
- Coupled electrical oscillators.
- Motion of atoms in a crystal.
- Vibrations of molecules.

In such systems, one coordinate is insufficient to describe the complete state of the system.

We therefore introduce a coordinate vector

$$
\mathbf{u}(t)=
\begin{pmatrix}
u_1(t)\\
u_2(t)\\
\vdots\\
u_n(t)
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The general equations of motion become

$$
\boxed{\ddot{u}_i=f_i(\mathbf{u},\dot{\mathbf{u}},t)}
$$

where $i=1,2,\ldots,n$.

For $p$ particles moving in three dimensions, the maximum number of coordinates is $n=3p$.

For two particles constrained to move along a straight line, $n=2$.

**Important terminology**

- Degree of freedom: An independent coordinate required to specify the configuration of a system.
- Equilibrium: A configuration in which all accelerations vanish and the system can remain stationary.
- Coupled motion: Motion in which the acceleration of one coordinate depends on other coordinates.

The purpose of this chapter is to develop the mathematical tools needed to analyse such systems.

# 2. Functions of several variables

Consider a function

$$
f=f(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n).
$$

Unlike a function of one variable, its value depends on several independent variables.

For example, the gravitational potential energy of a particle in a uniform gravitational field near Earth's surface is

$$
V(x,y,z)=mgz.
$$

The electrostatic potential generated by a point charge is

$$
V(x,y,z)=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}
\frac{q}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}}.
$$

In both cases, the function depends on spatial coordinates.

## 2.1 Partial derivatives

A partial derivative describes how a function changes when one variable changes while all other variables remain fixed.

For a function $f(x,y)$,

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
=
\lim_{\Delta x\rightarrow 0}
\frac{f(x+\Delta x,y)-f(x,y)}{\Delta x}
}
$$

Similarly,

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}
=
\lim_{\Delta y\rightarrow 0}
\frac{f(x,y+\Delta y)-f(x,y)}{\Delta y}.
$$

The distinction is important:

- $\partial f/\partial x$: Change in $f$ along the $x$-direction.
- $\partial f/\partial y$: Change in $f$ along the $y$-direction.

### Example 1: Temperature distribution

Suppose the temperature in a room is described by

$$
T(x,y)=20+3x^2+2y.
$$

Here, $x$ and $y$ are measured in metres and temperature is in °C.

Calculate the partial derivatives.

**Solution**

Differentiating with respect to $x$, keeping $y$ constant:

$$
\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}=6x.
$$

Similarly,

$$
\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}=2.
$$

At the point $(2,3)$,

$$
\left.\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}\right|_{(2,3)}
=12\ ^\circ\mathrm C/\mathrm m
$$

and

$$
\left.\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}\right|_{(2,3)}
=2\ ^\circ\mathrm C/\mathrm m.
$$

**Physical interpretation:** At this location, temperature increases more rapidly in the $x$-direction than in the $y$-direction.

## 2.2 Rules of partial differentiation

The usual differentiation rules apply to partial derivatives.

**Linearity**

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(af+bg)
=
a\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
+b\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}.
$$

**Product rule**

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\partial(fg)}{\partial x}
=
f\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}
+g\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
}
$$

The other independent variables are held constant during differentiation.

# 3. Chain rule for multivariable functions

This is one of the most important concepts in the chapter.

Suppose a function depends on several variables, and those variables themselves change with time.

For example,

$$
f=f(x,y)
$$

where

$$
x=x(t),\qquad y=y(t).
$$

The function $f$ changes with time because both $x$ and $y$ change.

Using the chain rule,

$$
\boxed{
\frac{df}{dt}
=
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt}
+
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}
}
$$

In dot notation,

$$
\boxed{
\frac{df}{dt}
=
\dot{x}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
+
\dot{y}\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}
}
$$

For $n$ variables,

$$
\frac{df}{dt}
=
\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\dot{x}_i\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}.
$$

### Example 2: Particle moving through a temperature field

Consider

$$
T(x,y)=20+3x^2+2y.
$$

A particle moves according to

$$
x(t)=2t,\qquad y(t)=t^2.
$$

Find the rate of change of temperature experienced by the particle at $t=1$.

**Solution**

First calculate the spatial derivatives:

$$
\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}=6x,
\qquad
\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}=2.
$$

The particle velocities are

$$
\dot{x}=2,\qquad \dot{y}=2t.
$$

Using the chain rule,

$$
\frac{dT}{dt}
=
6x\dot{x}+2\dot{y}.
$$

At $t=1$,

$$
x=2,\qquad \dot{x}=2,\qquad \dot{y}=2.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\frac{dT}{dt}
=6(2)(2)+2(2).
$$

$$
\boxed{\frac{dT}{dt}=28\ ^\circ\mathrm C/\mathrm s}
$$

The particle experiences a temperature increase of $28\ ^\circ\mathrm C$ per second at that instant.

## 3.1 Gradient

The partial derivatives can be collected into a vector called the gradient.

For a function $f(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n)$,

$$
\boxed{
\nabla f=
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}\\
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}\\
\vdots\\
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}
\end{pmatrix}
}
$$

In three-dimensional Cartesian coordinates,

$$
\boxed{
\nabla f=
\hat{\mathbf{x}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
+
\hat{\mathbf{y}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}
+
\hat{\mathbf{z}}\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}
}
$$

The gradient has two important properties:

1. Its direction is the direction of maximum increase of the function.
2. Its magnitude gives the maximum rate of increase per unit distance.

The chain rule can now be written compactly as

$$
\boxed{
\frac{df}{dt}=\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot\nabla f
}
$$

This is called differentiation along a curve.

### Example 3: Gradient of a temperature field

Consider

$$
T(x,y,z)=100-2x^2-3y^2-z^2.
$$

Calculate the gradient at $(1,2,1)$.

**Solution**

$$
\nabla T=
-4x\hat{\mathbf{x}}
-6y\hat{\mathbf{y}}
-2z\hat{\mathbf{z}}.
$$

At the specified point,

$$
\boxed{
\nabla T=-4\hat{\mathbf{x}}
-12\hat{\mathbf{y}}
-2\hat{\mathbf{z}}
}
$$

Its magnitude is

$$
|\nabla T|
=\sqrt{(-4)^2+(-12)^2+(-2)^2}
$$

$$
\boxed{|\nabla T|=\sqrt{164}=12.81\ ^\circ\mathrm C/\mathrm m}
$$

The direction of this gradient corresponds to the direction in which temperature increases most rapidly.

The direction of heat flow in an ordinary isotropic material is opposite to the temperature gradient, according to Fourier's law:

$$
\mathbf q=-k\nabla T.
$$

This is a useful physical application of the gradient.

## 3.2 Gradient of some common functions

For a constant vector $\mathbf a$ and position vector $\mathbf r$,

$$
\boxed{\nabla(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf r)=\mathbf a}
$$

$$
\boxed{\nabla(\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf r)=2\mathbf r}
$$

Since

$$
|\mathbf r|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2},
$$

we obtain

$$
\boxed{
\nabla|\mathbf r|
=
\frac{\mathbf r}{|\mathbf r|}
=\hat{\mathbf r}
}
$$

For a function depending only on radial distance,

$$
f=f(r),
$$

the gradient is

$$
\boxed{\nabla f(r)=\hat{\mathbf r}\frac{df}{dr}}
$$

This result is particularly useful in central-force problems.

For example, the gradient of the gravitational potential

$$
V(r)=-\frac{GMm}{r}
$$

is

$$
\nabla V=\frac{GMm}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf r}.
$$

Since force is minus the gradient of potential energy,

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf F=-\nabla V
=-\frac{GMm}{r^2}\hat{\mathbf r}
}
$$

This gives the familiar gravitational force.

## 3.3 Functions that also depend explicitly on time

Consider

$$
f=f(\mathbf r,t).
$$

The function can change because:

- The particle moves through space.
- The function itself changes with time, even at a fixed position.

The total time derivative is

$$
\boxed{
\frac{df}{dt}
=
\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot\nabla f
+
\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}
}
$$

The first term describes the change due to motion through space.

The second term describes the explicit time dependence at a fixed position.

### Example 4: Time-dependent temperature field

Suppose

$$
T(x,t)=20+2x+3t.
$$

A particle moves with constant velocity

$$
x(t)=4t.
$$

Find the rate at which its temperature changes.

**Solution**

$$
\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}=2,
\qquad
\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=3.
$$

Since

$$
\dot{x}=4,
$$

we get

$$
\frac{dT}{dt}
=
\dot{x}\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}
+\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}.
$$

Thus,

$$
\boxed{\frac{dT}{dt}=4(2)+3=11\ ^\circ\mathrm C/\mathrm s}
$$

# 4. Coordinate transformations and Jacobian matrices

A physical problem can often be described using different coordinate systems.

For example, a point in a plane can be described by:

- Cartesian coordinates: $(x,y)$.
- Polar coordinates: $(\rho,\phi)$.

The two descriptions are related through

$$
x=\rho\cos\phi,
\qquad
y=\rho\sin\phi.
$$

A coordinate transformation expresses one set of coordinates in terms of another.

## 4.1 Jacobian matrix

Suppose

$$
x_i=x_i(y_1,y_2,\ldots,y_n).
$$

The Jacobian matrix is defined as

$$
\boxed{
J_x=
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{\partial x_1}{\partial y_1}&\cdots&
\frac{\partial x_1}{\partial y_n}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{\partial x_n}{\partial y_1}&\cdots&
\frac{\partial x_n}{\partial y_n}
\end{pmatrix}
}
$$

It describes how small changes in the new coordinates produce changes in the old coordinates.

For a scalar function $f$,

$$
\boxed{
\nabla_y f=J_x^T\nabla_x f
}
$$

where $J_x^T$ is the transpose of the Jacobian.

The determinant of the Jacobian is called the Jacobian determinant.

A coordinate transformation is locally invertible when

$$
\boxed{\det J_x\neq 0}
$$

This condition ensures that the transformation can be reversed locally.

## 4.2 Polar coordinates

Polar coordinates are useful when a problem has circular symmetry.

The Cartesian and polar coordinates are related by

$$
\boxed{
x=\rho\cos\phi,\qquad
y=\rho\sin\phi
}
$$

where:

- $\rho$ is the distance from the origin.
- $\phi$ is the angle measured counterclockwise from the positive $x$-axis.

The Jacobian matrix is

$$
J=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos\phi&-\rho\sin\phi\\
\sin\phi&\rho\cos\phi
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its determinant is

$$
\boxed{\det J=\rho}
$$

Therefore, the transformation is locally invertible for $\rho>0$.

At the origin, the angle is undefined.

### Area element in polar coordinates

The infinitesimal area element transforms according to the Jacobian determinant:

$$
\boxed{dA=\rho\,d\rho\,d\phi}
$$

This is important when calculating areas, masses and moments of inertia.

### Example 5: Area of a circular region

Calculate the area of a circular disk of radius $2$ m using polar coordinates.

**Solution**

$$
A=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2 \rho\,d\rho\,d\phi.
$$

Integrating,

$$
A=\int_0^{2\pi}
\left[\frac{\rho^2}{2}\right]_0^2d\phi.
$$

$$
A=\int_0^{2\pi}2\,d\phi.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{A=4\pi=12.57\ \mathrm{m^2}}
$$

This agrees with the familiar result $A=\pi R^2$.

## 4.3 Velocity in polar coordinates

The position vector is

$$
\mathbf r=
\rho\cos\phi\,\hat{\mathbf x}
+
\rho\sin\phi\,\hat{\mathbf y}.
$$

Define the radial and angular unit vectors:

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol\rho}
=
\cos\phi\,\hat{\mathbf x}
+\sin\phi\,\hat{\mathbf y},
$$

$$
\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}
=
-\sin\phi\,\hat{\mathbf x}
+\cos\phi\,\hat{\mathbf y}.
$$

Differentiating the position vector gives

$$
\boxed{
\dot{\mathbf r}
=
\dot{\rho}\hat{\boldsymbol\rho}
+
\rho\dot{\phi}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}
}
$$

Since the two unit vectors are perpendicular,

$$
\boxed{
v^2=\dot{\rho}^{\,2}+\rho^2\dot{\phi}^{\,2}
}
$$

The gradient in polar coordinates is

$$
\boxed{
\nabla f=
\hat{\boldsymbol\rho}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\rho}
+
\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}\frac{1}{\rho}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi}
}
$$

### Example 6: Speed of a satellite in polar coordinates

A satellite moves in a circular orbit of radius $7000$ km with angular velocity

$$
\dot{\phi}=0.0011\ \mathrm{rad/s}.
$$

Find its speed.

**Solution**

For circular motion,

$$
\dot{\rho}=0.
$$

Therefore,

$$
v=\rho\dot{\phi}.
$$

Substituting,

$$
v=7.0\times10^6(0.0011).
$$

$$
\boxed{v=7700\ \mathrm{m/s}=7.7\ \mathrm{km/s}}
$$

This demonstrates why polar coordinates are convenient for circular motion.

## 4.4 Cylindrical coordinates

Cylindrical coordinates extend polar coordinates to three dimensions.

They are defined as

$$
\boxed{
x=\rho\cos\phi,\quad
y=\rho\sin\phi,\quad
z=z
}
$$

Here:

- $\rho$: Distance from the $z$-axis.
- $\phi$: Azimuthal angle.
- $z$: Height along the $z$-axis.

The Jacobian determinant is

$$
\boxed{J=\rho}
$$

The volume element becomes

$$
\boxed{dV=\rho\,d\rho\,d\phi\,dz}
$$

The velocity is

$$
\boxed{
\dot{\mathbf r}
=
\dot{\rho}\hat{\boldsymbol\rho}
+
\rho\dot{\phi}\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}
+
\dot z\hat{\mathbf z}
}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
v^2=\dot{\rho}^{\,2}+\rho^2\dot{\phi}^{\,2}+\dot z^2
}
$$

The gradient is

$$
\boxed{
\nabla f=
\hat{\boldsymbol\rho}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\rho}
+
\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}\frac{1}{\rho}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi}
+
\hat{\mathbf z}\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}
}
$$

**Physical applications**

Cylindrical coordinates are particularly useful for:

- Electric fields of infinitely long charged wires.
- Magnetic fields around current-carrying wires.
- Fluid flow through pipes.
- Rotating cylinders.
- Problems involving axial symmetry.

### Example 7: Volume of a cylinder

A cylinder has radius $0.5$ m and height $2$ m.

Calculate its volume using cylindrical coordinates.

**Solution**

$$
V=\int_0^2\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{0.5}
\rho\,d\rho\,d\phi\,dz.
$$

Performing the integrations,

$$
V=2(2\pi)\left[\frac{\rho^2}{2}\right]_0^{0.5}.
$$

$$
\boxed{V=0.5\pi=1.571\ \mathrm{m^3}}
$$

## 4.5 Spherical coordinates

Spherical coordinates are useful for systems with radial symmetry.

They are defined by

$$
\boxed{
x=r\sin\theta\cos\phi
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
y=r\sin\theta\sin\phi
}
$$

$$
\boxed{
z=r\cos\theta
}
$$

Here:

- $r$: Distance from the origin.
- $\theta$: Polar angle measured from the positive $z$-axis.
- $\phi$: Azimuthal angle measured in the $xy$-plane from the positive $x$-axis.

The Jacobian determinant is

$$
\boxed{J=r^2\sin\theta}
$$

The volume element is

$$
\boxed{
dV=r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi
}
$$

The velocity is

$$
\boxed{
\dot{\mathbf r}
=
\dot r\hat{\mathbf r}
+r\dot\theta\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}
+r\sin\theta\dot\phi\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}
}
$$

Consequently,

$$
\boxed{
v^2=
\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2
+r^2\sin^2\theta\dot\phi^2
}
$$

The gradient is

$$
\boxed{
\nabla f=
\hat{\mathbf r}\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}
+
\hat{\boldsymbol\theta}\frac{1}{r}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\theta}
+
\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi}
}
$$

**Physical applications**

- Gravitational fields of spherical bodies.
- Electrostatic fields of point charges.
- Atomic orbitals.
- Spherically symmetric charge distributions.
- Molecular and atomic physics.

### Example 8: Volume of a sphere

Calculate the volume of a sphere of radius $R$.

**Solution**

$$
V=\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_0^R
r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi.
$$

Separating the integrations,

$$
V=
\left[\frac{r^3}{3}\right]_0^R
\left[-\cos\theta\right]_0^\pi
\left[\phi\right]_0^{2\pi}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
V=\frac{R^3}{3}(2)(2\pi).
$$

$$
\boxed{V=\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3}
$$

# 5. Second-order partial derivatives and Schwarz's theorem

We can differentiate a multivariable function more than once.

For example,

$$
f(x,y)=x^2y+3xy^2.
$$

First-order derivatives:

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
=2xy+3y^2
$$

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}
=x^2+6xy.
$$

Second-order derivatives:

$$
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}=2y
$$

$$
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}=6x
$$

Mixed derivatives:

$$
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y\partial x}
=2x+6y
$$

$$
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x\partial y}
=2x+6y.
$$

For a sufficiently smooth function, Schwarz's theorem states that

$$
\boxed{
\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}
=
\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_j\partial x_i}
}
$$

In other words, the order of differentiation does not matter when the relevant second derivatives are continuous in a neighbourhood.

This property is important because it makes the Hessian matrix symmetric.

# 6. Taylor expansion for multivariable functions

Taylor expansion approximates a function near a specified point.

For a single-variable function,

$$
f(x)\approx f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)
+\frac12f''(a)(x-a)^2.
$$

For a function of several variables, we use the gradient and Hessian.

For a function $f(\mathbf x)$, expanded around $\mathbf y$,

$$
\boxed{
f(\mathbf x)=
f(\mathbf y)
+(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)\cdot\nabla f(\mathbf y)
+\frac12(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)^T
H_f(\mathbf y)(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)
+O(|\mathbf x-\mathbf y|^3)
}
$$

The Hessian matrix is

$$
\boxed{
H_f=
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_1^2}
&
\cdots
&
\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_1\partial x_n}
\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_n\partial x_1}
&
\cdots
&
\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x_n^2}
\end{pmatrix}
}
$$

It contains all the second-order partial derivatives.

For a function of two variables,

$$
H_f=
\begin{pmatrix}
f_{xx}&f_{xy}\\
f_{yx}&f_{yy}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Since mixed derivatives are equal for sufficiently smooth functions,

$$
f_{xy}=f_{yx}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
H_f=H_f^T.
$$

The Hessian is a real symmetric matrix.

## 6.1 Physical meaning of the Hessian

The Hessian describes the curvature of a function.

For example, consider potential energy $V(x,y)$.

The first derivatives determine the force:

$$
F_x=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial x},
\qquad
F_y=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial y}.
$$

The second derivatives describe how the force changes with displacement.

Thus, the Hessian of potential energy is closely related to the stiffness matrix of a mechanical system.

This is particularly important in vibration theory and coupled oscillations.

### Example 9: Hessian of a potential energy function

Consider

$$
V(x,y)=3x^2+2xy+4y^2.
$$

Find the Hessian.

**Solution**

First derivatives:

$$
V_x=6x+2y,
$$

$$
V_y=2x+8y.
$$

Second derivatives:

$$
V_{xx}=6,\qquad V_{yy}=8,
$$

$$
V_{xy}=V_{yx}=2.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
H_V=
\begin{pmatrix}
6&2\\
2&8
\end{pmatrix}
}
$$

The nonzero off-diagonal elements indicate that the two coordinates are coupled in the quadratic potential energy.

# 7. Equilibrium points and classification of extrema

Consider a function $f(x,y)$.

A stationary point occurs when

$$
\boxed{
\nabla f=0
}
$$

or equivalently,

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=0,
\qquad
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=0.
$$

However, a stationary point does not necessarily represent a minimum.

It may be:

1. A local minimum.
2. A local maximum.
3. A saddle point.

To determine the nature of the stationary point, we examine the Hessian matrix.

## 7.1 Eigenvalues of the Hessian

Because the Hessian is real and symmetric, it can be diagonalized using an orthogonal transformation:

$$
H=O^TDO
$$

where

$$
D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\ldots,\lambda_n).
$$

The quadratic contribution to the Taylor expansion becomes

$$
\frac12\sum_i\lambda_i z_i'^2.
$$

The signs of the eigenvalues determine the nature of the stationary point.

| Eigenvalues of Hessian | Nature of stationary point |
|---|---|
| All positive | Local minimum |
| All negative | Local maximum |
| Both positive and negative | Saddle point |
| One or more zero, with no opposite signs | Inconclusive from the quadratic test |

For two variables, one can also use the determinant and trace:

$$
D=f_{xx}f_{yy}-f_{xy}^2.
$$

At a stationary point:

- $D>0$, $f_{xx}>0$: Local minimum.
- $D>0$, $f_{xx}<0$: Local maximum.
- $D<0$: Saddle point.
- $D=0$: Test inconclusive.

## 7.2 Physical interpretation: Stability of equilibrium

This is an important connection to mechanics.

Consider a system with potential energy $V(x)$.

The force is

$$
F=-\frac{dV}{dx}.
$$

At equilibrium,

$$
\frac{dV}{dx}=0.
$$

For a stable equilibrium,

$$
\boxed{\frac{d^2V}{dx^2}>0}
$$

For an unstable equilibrium,

$$
\boxed{\frac{d^2V}{dx^2}<0}
$$

A stable equilibrium corresponds to a local minimum of potential energy, whereas an unstable equilibrium corresponds to a local maximum.

In multiple dimensions, the corresponding criterion is that the Hessian of the potential energy be positive definite for a strict local minimum.

### Example 10: Potential energy of a two-dimensional system

Consider

$$
V(x,y)=x^2+2y^2.
$$

Find the equilibrium point and determine its stability.

**Solution**

The first derivatives are

$$
V_x=2x,\qquad V_y=4y.
$$

At equilibrium,

$$
2x=0,\qquad 4y=0.
$$

Thus,

$$
\boxed{(x_0,y_0)=(0,0)}
$$

The Hessian is

$$
H_V=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&0\\
0&4
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its eigenvalues are

$$
\lambda_1=2,\qquad\lambda_2=4.
$$

Both are positive.

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{\text{The equilibrium is a stable local minimum.}}
$$

### Example 11: Identifying a saddle point

Consider

$$
V(x,y)=x^2-y^2.
$$

Find the stationary point and classify it.

**Solution**

$$
V_x=2x,\qquad V_y=-2y.
$$

The stationary point is

$$
(x_0,y_0)=(0,0).
$$

The Hessian is

$$
H_V=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&0\\
0&-2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its eigenvalues are

$$
\lambda_1=2,\qquad\lambda_2=-2.
$$

One is positive and the other is negative.

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{\text{The origin is a saddle point.}}
$$

A small displacement along the $x$-direction increases the potential energy, whereas a displacement along the $y$-direction decreases it.

The equilibrium is unstable.

# 8. Geometrical interpretation of the gradient

The gradient has a useful geometrical interpretation.

Consider a surface described by

$$
z=f(x,y).
$$

The gradient is

$$
\nabla f=
\begin{pmatrix}
f_x\\
f_y
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

It points in the direction of maximum increase of the function.

Its magnitude is

$$
\boxed{
|\nabla f|=
\sqrt{f_x^2+f_y^2}
}
$$

This gives the maximum directional slope.

For a unit vector $\hat{\mathbf n}$, the directional derivative is

$$
\boxed{
D_{\hat{\mathbf n}}f=
\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\nabla f
}
$$

The maximum value occurs when $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is parallel to the gradient.

### Example 12: Directional derivative

Consider

$$
f(x,y)=x^2+3y^2.
$$

Calculate the directional derivative at $(1,2)$ along the direction

$$
\mathbf a=3\hat{\mathbf x}+4\hat{\mathbf y}.
$$

**Solution**

First,

$$
\nabla f=
2x\hat{\mathbf x}+6y\hat{\mathbf y}.
$$

At $(1,2)$,

$$
\nabla f=2\hat{\mathbf x}+12\hat{\mathbf y}.
$$

The unit vector along $\mathbf a$ is

$$
\hat{\mathbf n}
=\frac{3\hat{\mathbf x}+4\hat{\mathbf y}}{5}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
D_{\hat{\mathbf n}}f
=
(2,12)\cdot\left(\frac35,\frac45\right).
$$

$$
=\frac65+\frac{48}{5}.
$$

$$
\boxed{D_{\hat{\mathbf n}}f=10.8}
$$

# 9. Level sets and the orthogonality of the gradient

A level set is a collection of points at which a function has a constant value.

For a function $f(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$,

$$
\boxed{
L_{f,c}=
\{\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^n:f(\mathbf x)=c\}
}
$$

Examples:

- In two dimensions, level sets are generally curves.
- In three dimensions, level sets are generally surfaces.
- For a function describing height, level sets correspond to contour lines on a topographic map.
- For electric potential, level sets are equipotential surfaces.

### Example 13: Circular level sets

Consider

$$
f(x,y)=x^2+y^2.
$$

The level set for $f=9$ is

$$
x^2+y^2=9.
$$

This is a circle of radius $3$.

The gradient is

$$
\nabla f=2x\hat{\mathbf x}+2y\hat{\mathbf y}.
$$

At every point on the circle, the gradient points radially outward.

The tangent to the circle is perpendicular to the radial direction.

Therefore, the gradient is perpendicular to the level curve.

## 9.1 Why is the gradient perpendicular to a level set?

Consider a curve $\mathbf r(t)$ lying entirely on a level set.

Along this curve,

$$
f(\mathbf r(t))=c
$$

where $c$ is constant.

Differentiating with respect to time,

$$
\frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r(t))=0.
$$

Using the chain rule,

$$
\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot\nabla f=0.
$$

Since $\dot{\mathbf r}$ is tangent to the curve,

$$
\boxed{
\nabla f\perp\text{ tangent to the level set}
}
$$

Thus, the gradient is normal to the level curve or level surface.

**Physical application: Electrostatics**

The electric field is

$$
\mathbf E=-\nabla V.
$$

Therefore, the electric field is perpendicular to equipotential surfaces.

This is why no work is done when a charge moves along an equipotential surface.

# 10. Linearization around equilibrium points

This section connects the mathematical concepts developed above to the dynamics of physical systems.

Consider a general system of equations:

$$
\boxed{
\ddot u_i=f_i(\mathbf u,\dot{\mathbf u})
}
$$

We assume that the equations have no explicit time dependence.

Suppose the system has a stationary equilibrium configuration

$$
\mathbf u=\mathbf u_0.
$$

At equilibrium, the velocity and acceleration are zero.

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
f_i(\mathbf u_0,\mathbf 0)=0
}
$$

Now consider a small displacement from equilibrium.

Write

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf u(t)=\mathbf u_0+\mathbf w(t)
}
$$

where $\mathbf w(t)$ is a small perturbation.

Since $\mathbf u_0$ is constant,

$$
\dot{\mathbf u}=\dot{\mathbf w},
\qquad
\ddot{\mathbf u}=\ddot{\mathbf w}.
$$

## 10.1 First-order Taylor expansion

Expand $f_i$ around the equilibrium point $(\mathbf u_0,\mathbf 0)$.

For small perturbations,

$$
f_i(\mathbf u_0+\mathbf w,\dot{\mathbf w})
\approx
f_i(\mathbf u_0,\mathbf 0)
+
\sum_j
\left[
w_j\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial u_j}
+
\dot w_j\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial\dot u_j}
\right]_0.
$$

The subscript $0$ indicates that the derivatives are evaluated at equilibrium.

But

$$
f_i(\mathbf u_0,\mathbf 0)=0.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\ddot w_i=
\sum_j
\left[
w_j\left.\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial u_j}\right|_0
+
\dot w_j\left.\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial\dot u_j}\right|_0
\right]
}
$$

This is the first-order linearized equation of motion.

Higher-order terms in the perturbations have been neglected.

## 10.2 Matrix representation

Define two matrices:

$$
A_{ij}=
\left.\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial u_j}\right|_0
$$

and

$$
B_{ij}=
\left.\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial\dot u_j}\right|_0.
$$

The equations become

$$
\boxed{
\ddot{\mathbf w}=A\mathbf w+B\dot{\mathbf w}
}
$$

This is a system of linear differential equations.

The matrices $A$ and $B$ describe how the acceleration responds to small changes in displacement and velocity.

This is the central mathematical step in analysing small oscillations around equilibrium.

## 10.3 Physical example: A single mass attached to a spring

Consider a mass $m$ attached to a spring of stiffness $k$.

Its equation of motion is

$$
m\ddot x=-kx.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\ddot x=-\frac{k}{m}x.
$$

The equilibrium is

$$
x_0=0.
$$

Define a small displacement

$$
x=x_0+w=w.
$$

The equation becomes

$$
\ddot w=-\frac{k}{m}w.
$$

Thus,

$$
\boxed{
\ddot w+\omega^2w=0
}
$$

where

$$
\boxed{\omega=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}}
$$

is the angular frequency.

Notice that the spring equation is already linear. Linearization does not change it.

## 10.4 Physical example: Nonlinear spring

Consider a nonlinear spring described by

$$
m\ddot x=-kx-\alpha x^3.
$$

The equation is nonlinear because of the $x^3$ term.

At equilibrium,

$$
x_0=0.
$$

Write

$$
x=w.
$$

Then

$$
m\ddot w=-kw-\alpha w^3.
$$

For sufficiently small displacements, $w^3$ is much smaller than $w$. Neglecting the cubic term,

$$
m\ddot w\approx-kw.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\ddot w+\frac{k}{m}w=0
}
$$

The nonlinear system behaves approximately like a simple harmonic oscillator for sufficiently small displacements around equilibrium.

Its small-amplitude angular frequency is

$$
\boxed{\omega=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}}
$$

## 10.5 Connection to coupled oscillations

For several interacting particles, the linearized equations can be written in matrix form.

For a conservative mechanical system with kinetic energy described by a mass matrix $M$ and potential energy $V(\mathbf u)$, the equations near equilibrium take the form

$$
\boxed{
M\ddot{\mathbf w}+H_V\mathbf w=0
}
$$

where $H_V$ is the Hessian of the potential energy evaluated at equilibrium.

This expression assumes constant mass matrix and a sufficiently smooth potential.

It reveals the connection between two important concepts:

- The Hessian describes the curvature of the potential energy.
- The Hessian determines the restoring forces responsible for small oscillations.

The normal-mode frequencies can be obtained from the generalized eigenvalue equation

$$
\boxed{
H_V\mathbf a=\omega^2M\mathbf a
}
$$

This last equation is an extension of the supplied notes, which stop at the first-order linearization in Eq. (5.48). It is the natural next step toward a full treatment of coupled oscillations.

# 12. Additional numerical exercises for students

These exercises are suitable for a university-level tutorial or the last 15–20 minutes of the lecture.

## Exercise 1: Partial differentiation

The potential energy of a particle is

$$
V(x,y)=5x^2+3xy+2y^2.
$$

Calculate:

(a) $\partial V/\partial x$

(b) $\partial V/\partial y$

(c) Both derivatives at $(2,1)$.

**Answer:**

$$
V_x=10x+3y,\qquad V_y=3x+4y.
$$

At $(2,1)$:

$$
\boxed{V_x=23,\qquad V_y=10}
$$

## Exercise 2: Chain rule in mechanics

A particle moves according to

$$
x(t)=3t^2,\qquad y(t)=2t.
$$

Its potential energy is

$$
V(x,y)=x^2+xy.
$$

Calculate $dV/dt$ at $t=1$.

**Answer:**

$$
\frac{dV}{dt}
=
(2x+y)\dot x+x\dot y.
$$

At $t=1$:

$$
x=3,\quad y=2,\quad\dot x=6,\quad\dot y=2.
$$

$$
\boxed{\frac{dV}{dt}=50}
$$

## Exercise 3: Gradient and force

A particle has potential energy

$$
V(x,y,z)=4x^2+2y^2+3z^2.
$$

Calculate the force at $(1,2,1)$.

**Answer:**

$$
\mathbf F=-\nabla V.
$$

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf F=-8\hat{\mathbf x}
-8\hat{\mathbf y}
-6\hat{\mathbf z}
}
$$

## Exercise 4: Polar-coordinate motion

A particle moves in a plane with

$$
\rho(t)=2t,\qquad \phi(t)=3t.
$$

Calculate its speed at $t=2$.

**Answer:**

$$
v^2=\dot\rho^2+\rho^2\dot\phi^2.
$$

At $t=2$:

$$
\rho=4,\quad\dot\rho=2,\quad\dot\phi=3.
$$

$$
v=\sqrt{2^2+4^2(3^2)}
$$

$$
\boxed{v=\sqrt{148}=12.17\ \mathrm{m/s}}
$$

## Exercise 5: Jacobian

Consider the coordinate transformation

$$
x=2u+v,\qquad y=u-3v.
$$

Calculate the Jacobian determinant.

**Answer:**

$$
J=
\begin{pmatrix}
2&1\\
1&-3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

$$
\boxed{\det J=-7}
$$

Since the determinant is nonzero, the transformation is locally invertible everywhere.

## Exercise 6: Hessian and stability

Consider

$$
V(x,y)=2x^2+4xy+5y^2.
$$

(a) Find the stationary point.

(b) Calculate the Hessian.

(c) Determine the stability.

**Answer:**

The stationary point is

$$
\boxed{(0,0)}
$$

The Hessian is

$$
H=
\begin{pmatrix}
4&4\\
4&10
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its determinant is

$$
D=40-16=24>0.
$$

Since $V_{xx}=4>0$, the stationary point is a local minimum.

$$
\boxed{\text{Stable equilibrium}}
$$

## Exercise 7: Real spring system

A mass of $0.5$ kg is attached to a spring with spring constant $200$ N/m.

Calculate:

(a) Its angular frequency.

(b) Its frequency.

(c) Its period.

**Answer:**

$$
\omega=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}
=\sqrt{\frac{200}{0.5}}
=20\ \mathrm{rad/s}.
$$

$$
f=\frac{\omega}{2\pi}=3.18\ \mathrm{Hz}.
$$

$$
\boxed{T=\frac1f=0.314\ \mathrm{s}}
$$

## Exercise 8: Coupled potential energy

Consider two generalized coordinates with potential energy

$$
V(x,y)=\frac12(4x^2+2xy+3y^2).
$$

(a) Calculate the Hessian.

(b) Find its eigenvalues.

(c) Determine whether the equilibrium at the origin is stable.

**Answer:**

$$
H=
\begin{pmatrix}
4&1\\
1&3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The eigenvalues are

$$
\lambda_{\pm}
=
\frac{7\pm\sqrt5}{2}.
$$

Both eigenvalues are positive.

Therefore,
  
$$
\boxed{\text{The origin is a stable equilibrium.}}
$$

The nonzero off-diagonal elements indicate coupling between the coordinates.

# 13. Suggested lecture sequence

For a 90-minute university lecture, the following sequence is appropriate.

| Time | Topic |
|---|---|
| 0–8 min | Introduction and physical motivation for multiple coordinates |
| 8–18 min | Partial derivatives |
| 18–30 min | Chain rule and gradient |
| 30–45 min | Coordinate transformations and Jacobians |
| 45–57 min | Polar, cylindrical and spherical coordinates |
| 57–68 min | Taylor expansion and Hessian |
| 68–78 min | Equilibrium, stability and level sets |
| 78–90 min | Linearization and physical examples |

**Three concepts to emphasize throughout the lecture:**

1. The gradient tells us how a physical quantity changes in space and identifies the direction of maximum increase.
2. The Hessian describes curvature and determines the local nature of a stationary point.
3. Linearization allows us to replace a complicated nonlinear dynamical system with a simpler linear system near equilibrium.

The third point is particularly important because it prepares students for the subsequent study of normal modes and coupled oscillations.
